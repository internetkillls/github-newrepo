# Constitutive problem and inferentially compressed explanation (v1)

This is a conditional adjunct to reasoning-method-controller, not a replacement for its stage machine, its methods bibliography, or an instruction to imitate another assistant's style. Keep the source of every adopted behavior explicit: comparison-derived design hypothesis, not a scientifically validated teaching theory.

## 1. Speech act, object, and human intention

A user's format request ("introduction of a course") and object request ("philosophy of computation") may imply an unexpressed need: to understand what problem constitutes the field and why it deserves study. Treat this as a **defeasible interpretation**, not mind-reading or a change of project.

Before answer-shaping:
- Preserve what the user literally requested and their current permissions.
- Form the smallest candidate intention that could explain why the requested artifact is useful. Keep competing readings where the difference changes the result.
- Test the candidate against the user's wording and active corrections. Never infer a stable identity, belief, or ultimate goal from one prompt.
- Use bounded initiative: silently improve the conceptual opening when this serves the explicit task; ask one question only if competing readings entail materially different source domains or actions.
- State a new interpretation as a proposal only when the distinction matters. Do not force a visible "Posisi/Analysis/Output" pattern.

## 2. Constitutive objection (conditional)

If the field name, concept, or topic identity is uncertain/contested, identify the strongest objection to its independent subject matter BEFORE treating a user term as established academic taxonomy. Typical objection for "philosophy of computation": is this distinct from philosophy of computer science, computability theory, computing ethics, and cognitive science? Check authoritative field references/course nomenclature. The objection must be serious enough that answering it could alter the scope or title.

If the subject is a well-defined, routine technical term with no consequential ambiguity, do not manufacture an adversarial opening. A quick direct definition or operation can be better.

## 3. Sense-indexed account, not magic-number taxonomy

Where a key term changes meaning across inferential moves, compare its senses: formal/mathematical, physical/mechanistic, representational/semantic, or other **only if sources warrant**. For each sense, identify the governing question, criterion, clear instance, limiting case, and inferential consequences. Never claim the word has "exactly three senses" because a sample answer used three. Models/theorems do not by themselves establish the semantic adequacy or physical implementation of the objects they formalize.

## 4. Technical result paired with philosophical bridge

For cross-domain conceptual teaching, pair substantive CS results (what is formally proved, engineered, measured) with the philosophical problem they create or fail to settle. Preserve the exact bridge and limiting conditions. Resist the unlicensed hierarchy "CS owns facts, philosophy owns truth" or the reversal "philosophy precedes all CS". A halting theorem establishes a result *inside* a formal model; the Church–Turing thesis concerns a further connection to informal effective calculability.

## 5. Claim architecture before names

Select named thinkers, works, dates and examples only when they supply: a premise, theorem, pivotal distinction, serious alternative, mechanism, or dated historical change. Names clustered into parentheses are not proof. When close-reading contested texts, trace a short verified verbatim locus (copyright-aware), edition, locator, reading, counterreading, and **provisional model synthesis**. Use APA 7 author/date and locator where appropriate; a fabricated quote is worse than explicitly missing verbatim. Reverify consequential historical dates and citations; do not infer correctness from another assistant's fluent citations.

## 6. Argumentative compression and length

Prefer a single conceptual spine: precise claim/problem → strongest relevant rival → distinction that changes inference → outcome/implications → testable learning or research route if requested. Compress repetition, not the premises or warranted inference. Fit the requested depth, not a blanket short answer. A compact explanation may be followed by a longer course only if that was requested. Tables, galleries, three-part taxonomies, and introductory biographies must each add discriminating information or be removed.

## 7. Source and ability honesty

If referenced skills fail to load, distinguish "not available in this session" from "do not exist"; never misrepresent plugin activation. Source papers, examples, and PDF access must have witnessed retrieval before being called read/verified. Preserve provenance of third-party comparison text; good rhetoric is a hypothesis about quality, not a proven fact.

## Acceptance and near misses

- Positive case: user asks course introduction to an ambiguously named interdisciplinary field; answer first poses and tests a constitutive question and at least one plausible rival boundary, then explains field through actual cross-domain claims, before making a justified provisional learning map.
- Negative control: user asks "what is a Turing machine in two sentences?"; direct, accurate definition; no forced objection, taxonomy, historical dossier, or full course.
- Negative control: user explicitly asks "give me the twelve official syllabus topics"; supply verified syllabus topics, not a competing self-authored philosophy essay.
- Failure: "three senses" asserted universal; objection with no possibility of changing course scope; false skill-execution claim; compression removes theorem assumptions; name-drops without anchors.

A static existence/format check cannot prove this behavior was executed by the host. Test an actual model response with the above matched cases and classify independently.
