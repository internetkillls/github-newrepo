# Semantic depth and conceptual descent

Use this module when a term bears argumentative weight but the current interpretation is being supplied mainly by lexical similarity, embeddings, synonyms, nearby topics, or continuity of wording.

This is a METHOD module. Its examples and sources do not become OBJECT content unless the target independently requires them.

## 1. Horizontal neighborhood versus structural load

A semantic neighborhood answers questions such as:
- which words are similar?
- which topics co-occur?
- which concepts are commonly associated?

That can help discovery, but it is not enough for theory-bearing terms.

Structural semantic load asks:
- what problem or question makes this term intelligible here?
- what presuppositions make the question possible?
- what relations/conditions constitute the concept in this use?
- what roles, institutions, practices, mechanisms, or artifacts realize it?
- what inferential commitments follow?
- what is excluded or contrasted?
- what breaks if this use is denied?
- what rival scheme uses the same term differently?

Lexical proximity is orientation evidence, not a warrant for the concept's structure.

## 2. Semantic descent operator

For an inference-bearing term T, descend only as far as needed:

### A. Use anchor
Identify whose use is at issue: user, source author, field, model, or local stipulation. Preserve version/historical context when it changes the use.

### B. Governing question
Recover the question/problem to which T functions as an answer, distinction, criterion, or presupposition.

This is the Collingwoodian contribution: a proposition/term is better understood when placed in the question–answer/presupposition structure that gives it logical work.

### C. Constitutive dependencies
Ask which relations/conditions must obtain for T to have this role. These may be logical, normative, institutional, causal, technical, social, or metaphysical depending on the object.

Do not use a universal checklist as answer content. Derive object-native dependencies.

### D. Material/practical realization
Where applicable, identify actors, practices, institutions, procedures, artifacts, resources, constraints, powers, or enforcement mechanisms through which the concept is instantiated.

"Material" here means moving from a compressed abstraction toward its operative conditions and realizations. It does not by itself commit the analysis to philosophical materialism.

### E. Inferential role
Ask what one becomes committed or entitled to infer if T is accepted in this sense, and what conclusions would no longer be licensed if the sense changes.

This is where inferential-role semantics and argument-scheme analysis are useful: meaning-bearing concepts constrain good/bad moves, hidden premises, warrants, and critical questions.

### F. Contrast and exclusion
Identify what T is not, which rival distinctions it excludes, and which counterexamples expose an overbroad use.

### G. Conceptual-scheme status
Distinguish:
- **descriptive reconstruction**: what structure is already embedded in the actual conceptual/practical scheme under study;
- **revisionary reconstruction**: a proposed reorganization, replacement, or improvement of that scheme.

This distinction is inspired by Strawson's descriptive/revisionary metaphysics. Do not assume descriptive reconstruction is metaphysically neutral or universally correct; it is a mode distinction.

### H. Elimination/residual test
When verbal dispute is possible, temporarily bracket or eliminate the disputed term and restate the substantive commitments. If the disagreement survives, the term was not the whole dispute. If it disappears, the dispute may be verbal or framework-relative.

This operation is licensed by the verbal-dispute tradition; do not turn the method source into the topic unless requested.

## 3. Framework fan-out before framework lock-in

Do not equate a heavy term with the first famous theory retrieved.

For terms such as justice, freedom, agency, rationality, delegation, control, autonomy, responsibility, representation, intelligence, or ownership:
1. identify the local problem;
2. map materially distinct candidate frameworks/senses;
3. test which framework best explains the current inferential role;
4. keep rivals live when the evidence does not discriminate.

Example of the rule, not a canonical mapping:
"justice" should not be expanded merely to nearby words such as fairness/equality. In a Rawlsian object-context, it may require the basic structure, fair terms of cooperation, the original-position device, and principles governing distribution; in another tradition its load may instead be legitimacy, consent, desert, recognition, capability, domination, or something else. The framework must be earned from the object.

## 4. Extensionality warning

Do not confuse this module's "extensive/deep unpacking" with the technical semantic notion of extension. The aim is not merely to list instances or denotations. The aim is to expose the dependency structure carried by the use.

## 5. Research interface

When the conceptual load is not recoverable from the supplied corpus:
- search the field-native terminology around the governing question, not synonyms alone;
- retrieve canonical/historical uses and serious rivals;
- inspect how the term is operationalized in practice/institutions/technical systems when relevant;
- preserve source-specific senses and dates;
- treat community usage as discovery signal unless stronger warrant exists.

## 6. Output behavior

The internal descent should normally surface as explanatory prose:
- explain why the term requires a particular condition;
- show how one dependency leads to another;
- distinguish rival uses where they alter the argument;
- avoid dumping the internal descent card or philosopher names as a checklist.

A successful answer makes the concept thicker without sounding like a taxonomy.

## 7. Warrants and limits

### Strawson — descriptive versus revisionary metaphysics
Public-access orientation: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Peter Frederick Strawson.”
Licensed operation: distinguish reconstruction of an existing conceptual scheme from a proposal to revise it.
Limit: Strawson's own claim about a shared central conceptual scheme is a substantive philosophical thesis, not a controller default.

### Collingwood — question/answer and presuppositional analysis
Public-access orientation: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Robin George Collingwood.”
Licensed operation: recover the question and presuppositions that give an assertion/concept its role.
Limit: do not assume every inquiry is best reconstructed through Collingwood's metaphysics.

### Frame semantics / encyclopedic meaning
Public-access orientation: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Word Meaning.”
Licensed operation: treat lexical expressions as access points into structured background knowledge/frames rather than isolated synonym sets.
Limit: frame semantics is one semantic tradition, not the ontology of all concepts.

### Inferentialist semantics
Public-access orientation: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Theories of Meaning.”
Licensed operation: inspect the inferential consequences and practical distinction between good/bad moves associated with a concept.
Limit: inferentialism is a theoretical option, not proof that all meaning reduces to inference.

### Chalmers — verbal disputes
BUNDLED user-supplied source: `assets/knowledge/verbal-disputes.pdf`.
Licensed operation: bracket/eliminate a term to test whether substantive disagreement survives.
Limit: term elimination diagnoses verbal contribution; it does not itself reconstruct the full material/metaphysical load.

### Walton/Reed/Macagno — argumentation schemes
BUNDLED user-supplied source: `assets/knowledge/argumentation-schemes-chapter.pdf`.
Licensed operation: expose recurring premise/warrant structures and critical questions.
Limit: a scheme classifies defeasible argumentative structure; it does not fix the substantive meaning of every term in the scheme.
